What is the Riemann hypothesis?

About 2 min readDraft · work in progressInteractive

February ?, 2026

One of the oldest theorems in mathematics is that there are infinitely many primes. Euclid (the geometer!) proved this around 300 BCE, and for centuries afterwards the story ended there. There are infinitely many primes, what else is there to ask?

However, some infinite sets are more `common' than others. For example, even numbers are pretty common, but powers of 2 are pretty rare: between \(1\) and \(1000,\) there are 500 even numbers, but only ten powers of 2.

To measure the size of an infinite set of numbers \(A\), it is useful to think about its natural density, defined as \[d(A) := \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{\#(\text{elements of \(A\) from \(1\) to \(N\)})}{N}.\]

For example, the natural density of the the even integers is \(1/2.\) This is because the number of even integers between \(1\) and \(n\) is always \(\lfloor n/2\rfloor,\) and \[\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}{n} = \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{n/2}{n} = \frac{1}{2}.\]

The natural density of the set of powers of \(2\), on the other hand, is zero. This is because between \(1\) and \(n,\) there are \(1 + \lfloor \log_2(n)\rfloor\) powers of 2, and \[\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1 + \lfloor \log_2(n) \rfloor}{n} = \lim_{n\to\infty} = 0,\] because the logarithm grows much much slower than \(n.\)

To get a feel for what natural density is expressing, playing with the following calculator might be helpful. For a number \(n,\) it tells you what percent of the numbers between \(1\) and \(n\) are even, and what percent are powers of 2.
