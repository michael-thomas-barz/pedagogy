Mechanics IV: from Hamiltonian to quantum mechanics

We introduce basic ideas in quantum mechanics and how they relate to Hamilton's approach to mechanics.

Level 4 Helpful: Linear algebra; Hamiltonian mechanics About 30 min read

October 2, 2026

In our series on mechanics, we discussed several approaches to mechanics, leading up to Hamilton’s approach. Although Hamilton’s approach is abstract, it is a more symmetric approach (treating position and momentum on equal grounds), and it helps us better understand conserved quantities. However, it has another big advantage: it serves as a bridge between classical and quantum mechanics.

Recall how, in the geometric approach to Hamiltonian mechanics, we showed how symplectic geometry allows you to phrase mechanics in terms of the geometry of phase spaces -- geometric spaces which encode the possible states of some physical system. These phase spaces are abstract shapes which are hard to 'see' directly; instead, scientists access them by making measurements (see our article on Gelfand duality for an elaboration on this).

In quantum mechanics, this view of physics reigns supreme, with one change: the phase spaces involved become much stranger than any usual shapes.

What is quantum mechanics?

Schrodinger’s cat

You may have heard of the famous thought experiment of Schrodinger’s cat, that the cat is both alive and dead simultaneously. What does that mean, really?

A key principle in quantum mechanics is that particles only exist probabilistically. In other words, instead of saying a particle is at position \((1,2)\), in quantum mechanics you say that the position of a particle is a normal distribution centered around \((1,2)\) with standard deviation \(3\). Observing the particle amounts to sampling this distribution: you are likely to observe the particle close to \((1,2)\), but with a very small probability, you may observe it at \((100,-9)\). In other words, the measurements scientists make behave more like random variables than like traditional functions.

Going back to the example of Schrodinger’s cat, you take a particle which could have two states A and B, and assume it could be each state with probability \(\frac12\). Then, you make a contraption to release poison to the cat only when the particle has state B (scientists seem to have quite cruel minds... why not have the theoretical contraption release candy?). If you do this, the cat will also be dead and alive with probability \(\frac12\).

There is an important distinction between the kind of probabilities occurring here versus the kind of probability people mean when they talk about flipping a coin.

For example, when you flip a coin, it also lands heads or tails with probability \(\frac12\). Perhaps you decide you'll flip a coin, and then release the poison only when the coin flips heads. How does this differ from the quantum situation?

The key is that if you knew the speed and force at which you flipped the coin, you could in principle know whether it would land heads or tails before the experiment. In other words, we only say it lands heads or tails with probability \(\frac12\) due to our ignorance. However, in quantum mechanics, a particle can have a physical state which is state A with probability \(\frac12\) and state B with probability \(\frac12\). Even an all-knowing being could not tell you which state the particle is in before observing it. So, Schrodinger’s cat is really both physically alive and dead, in a precise physical sense.

Wave functions

How do we actually express a particle in quantum mechanics? We will use wave functions. A wave function is a complex-valued function \(\psi(\vec x,t)\), where \(\vec x\in\mathbb R^3\) is the position and \(t\) is the time. When a particle has wave function \(\psi(\vec x,t)\), it means at time \(t\), you have probability \(|\psi(\vec x,t)|^2\) of finding it at position \(\vec x\). In particular, since probabilities should add to 1, we want \[ \int_{\mathbb R^3}|\psi(\vec x,t)|^2d\vec x=1. \]

The fundamental equation in quantum mechanics is Schrodinger’s equation, which states that if a particle of mass \(m\) is in a potential \(V(\vec x)\), the wave function obeys the differential equation \[ i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\psi(\vec x,t)=\bigg(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\vec x^2}+V(\vec x)\bigg)\psi(\vec x,t). \] Here, \(\hbar\) is called the (reduced) Planck constant, and it’s a very small number \[ \hbar\approx1.054\times 10^{-34}\mathrm{J}\cdot\mathrm{s}. \](which is why we only see quantum mechanics come into play on small scales). This is analogous to the equation for the energy \(E=\frac{p^2}{2m}+V,\) where the energy \(E\) is replaced by the operator \[ i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}, \] the momentum \(p\) is replaced by the operator \[ \frac\hbar i\frac\partial{\partial\vec x}. \]

A linear algebraic foundation to quantum mechanics

In the previous section, we saw how we can formulate quantum mechanics through wave functions \(\psi(\vec x,t)\), which describes the probability distribution of the position of a particle. However, we could equally have used wave functions \(\psi(\vec p,t)\) which describes the probability distribution of the momentum of a particle. This suggests wave functions may not be the best way to formalize quantum mechanics.

In the mathematical formalism of quantum mechanics developed by Paul Dirac, David Hilbert, John von Neumann, and Hermann Weyl, we instead think of a state of a quantum mechanical system as a vector \(|\psi\rangle\) in some vector space \(\mathcal H\). Our previous assumption that the probabilities in a wave function add to 1, \[ \int_{\mathbb R^3}|\psi(\vec x,t)|^2d\vec x=1, \] is replaced with the hypothesis that the vector \(|\psi\rangle\) has length 1. In Dirac’s notation, we write this as \[ \langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1. \] In general, given two vectors \(|\phi\rangle\) and \(|\psi\rangle\) in the vector space \(\mathcal H\), Dirac denotes their inner product as \[ \langle\phi|\psi\rangle. \] The physical state evolves with time, say as a function \(|\psi(t)\rangle\)--this is the Heisenberg picture.

How do we relate these vectors back to wave functions? First of all, in this formalism, observables, such as position, momentum, angular momentum, … are all linear operators on \(\mathcal H\). For example, position becomes some linear operator \(\hat x\). Let \(|\vec x\rangle\) be eigenvectors of this position operator (which exists for each position \(\vec x\)). Then, \[ \psi(\vec x,t)=\langle\vec x|\psi(t)\rangle. \] In other words, if we imagine expanding the vector \(|\psi(t)\rangle\) in terms of the eigenvectors \(|\vec x\rangle\), we can write it as \[ |\psi(t)\rangle=\sum_{\vec x\in\mathbb R^3}\psi(\vec x,t)|\vec x\rangle. \] Of course, this doesn’t literally make sense, since this is an infinite sum… Instead, we should write \[ |\psi(t)\rangle=\int_{\mathbb R^3}\psi(\vec x,t)|\vec x\rangle d\vec x. \] Recall that when we observe the particle, we will find it at position \(\vec x\) with probability \(|\psi(x,t)|^2\). In the language of linear algebra, we project the vector \(|\psi(t)\rangle\) to the eigenvector \(|\vec x\rangle\) with probability which is the absolute value squared of \(\langle\vec x|\psi(t)\rangle\).

We could similarly recover the wave function with respect to momentum, \(\psi(\vec p,t)\), by letting \(\hat p\) be the momentum operator, and letting \(|\vec p\rangle\) be eigenvectors. Then \[ \psi(\vec p,t)=\langle \vec p|\psi(t)\rangle, \] and we could do all the same things we did from before with position.

The uncertainty principle

One of the most fundamental principles in quantum mechanics is Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle. It states that you cannot know both the position and momentum of a particle precisely. This becomes particularly lucid when you think about quantum mechanics through linear operators.

Given a state \(|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal H\), observing the position will collapse the vector to some eigenvector of the position operator \(\hat x\). In other words, knowing the position of the particle exactly would mean the state \(|\psi\rangle\) is an eigenvector of \(\hat x\). Similarly, if you knew the momentum exactly, then \(|\psi\rangle\) would be an eigenvector of the momentum operator \(\hat p\).

Therefore, Heisenberg's uncertainty principle says that \(\hat x\) and \(\hat p\) have no simultaneous eigenvectors. Let's take a detour into linear algebra and think about when two matrices have simultaneous eigenvectors.

A detour: eigenvalues and eigenvectors

A matrix \(A\) has an eigenvalue \(\lambda\) if you have a non-zero vector \(\vec v\)--called an eigenvector--such that \[ A\vec v=\lambda\vec v. \] For example, the matrix \[ A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\ -2&3 \end{pmatrix} \] has eigenvalues \(\lambda=1,2\), since \[ A\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix},\ A\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}=2\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}. \]

An important result in linear algebra is that if \(A\) and \(B\) are two matrices which commute with each other, i.e., \[ AB=BA,\] then you can find a simultaneous eigenvector: some vector \(\vec v\) such that \[ A\vec v=\lambda\vec v,\ B\vec v=\mu\vec v\] for some scalars \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\).

The basic reason is because if \(\vec v\) is an eigenvector of \(A\), then \(B\vec v\) is also an eigenvector of \(A\). Indeed, if \(A\vec v=\lambda\vec v\) for some scalar \(\lambda\), then \[ A(B\vec v)=B(A\vec v)=B(\lambda\vec v)=\lambda(B\vec v), \] since \(A\) and \(B\) commute with each other. That means you can “arrange” an eigenvector for \(B\) without breaking the condition of being an eigenvector for \(A\).

To see a more rigorous proof, you can click here.

First of all, remember that any complex matrix has at least one eigenvalue. So let \(\lambda\) be an eigenvalue for \(A\). Then the vectors with eigenvalue \(\lambda\) form a subspace \[ V=\{\vec v\in\mathbb C^n:A\vec v=\lambda\vec v\}\subset\mathbb C^n. \] The matrix \(B\) preserves this subspace, in the sense that if \(\vec v\in V\) then \(B\vec v\in V\) as well. That’s because \[ A(B\vec v)=B(A\vec v)=B(\lambda\vec v)=\lambda(B\vec v). \] Now, \(B\) is a linear operator on \(V\), hence also has an eigenvector, call it \(\vec v\in V\), with some eigenvalue \(\mu\). This is the vector we were looking for.

Back to uncertainty

Therefore, the uncertainty principle tells us that the position and momentum operators \(\hat x\) and \(\hat p\) do not commute with each other. The extent to which the operators do not commute with each other is called the commutator, \[ [\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar. \]
