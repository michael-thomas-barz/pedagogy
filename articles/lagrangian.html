Why is momentum conserved?

July 10, 2026

Nature is lazy and always evolves in the most "efficient" way possible. This is why soap bubbles are spherical--to minimize surface area--and why light refracts when traveling through glass.

Lagrange's idea was to use this principle, that nature is lazy, as the foundations for mechanics. This is in contrast to Newton's approach to mechanics, which is based on force. Our goal in this article is to mathematically formalize Lagrange's idea.

Since Newton's approach to physics already works so well, you may ask why we care about Lagrange's approach. One reason is Emmy Noether's discovery that conservation laws (conservation of energy, momentum, and angular momentum) fall out naturally from Lagrange's approach to mechanics!

Newton's approach to physics

Let's imagine 3 particle with some masses \(m_1,m_2,m_3\) at some positions \(\vec x_1,\vec x_2,\vec x_3\) and having some velocities \(\vec v_1,\vec v_2,\vec v_3\). The fundamental equation in Newtonian mechanics is \(F=ma\). Let's say the entire system of particles experiences a force of \(\vec F\). That means \[ \vec F=m_1\frac{d\vec v_1}{dt}+m_2\frac{d\vec v_2}{dt}+m_3\frac{d\vec v_3}{dt}. \] Of course, we also know that \[ \vec v_1=\frac{d\vec x_1}{dt},\ \ \vec v_2=\frac{d\vec x_2}{dt},\ \ \vec v_3=\frac{d\vec x_3}{dt}. \] Newton would solve these equations to figure out how each particle moves.

Least action principle

Let's now try to understand what Lagrange's approach to physics is. Nature being "lazy" means it expends the least energy possible. So he defined a function \(L\), called the Lagrangian, which keeps track of all the energy present in the system. It is \[ L=T-V\] where \(T\) is kinetic energy, and \(V\) is potential energy. The kinetic energy is \[ T=\frac12m_1v_1^2+\frac12m_2v_2^2+\frac12m_3v_3^2, \] where by \(v_i\) we mean the size of the vector \(\vec v_i\). The function \(V\) can change depending on the context. For example, if the particles experience gravity, the gravitational potential \(mgh\) would appear here.

The important point is that \(L\) is some function of the positions \(\vec x_1,\vec x_2,\vec x_3\), their velocities \(\vec v_1,\vec v_2,\vec v_3\), and time \(t\). We might write it as \[ L(\vec x_1,\vec x_2,\vec x_3,\vec v_1,\vec v_2,\vec v_3,t).\] Our goal is to understand how the positions and velocities of the particles change over time.

Let's say the particles evolve as functions \(\vec x_1(t),\vec x_2(t),\vec x_3(t)\) and their velocities evolve as functions \(\vec v_1(t),\vec v_2(t),\vec v_3(t)\). Since we specified the positions and velocities of the particles at the beginning, we want \[ \vec x_1(0)=\vec x_1,\ \ \vec x_2(0)=\vec x_2,\ \ \vec x_3(0)=\vec x_3 \] and \[ \vec v_1(0)=\vec v_1,\ \ \vec v_2(0)=\vec v_2,\ \ \vec v_3(0)=\vec v_3. \] The total energy expended after evolving from time \(0\) to \(T\) with these functions is the integral, called the action, \[ S=\int_0^T L\big(\vec x_1(t),\vec x_2(t),\vec x_3(t),\vec v_1(t),\vec v_2(t),\vec v_3(t),t\big)dt. \] Therfore, a lazy universe would try to minimize the value of \(S\). How do we go about solving this?

You might know how to minimize a function, say \(x^2-3x\). But notice that we are trying to solve a much crazier problem right now! When we are trying to minimize \(x^2-3x\), we are allowed to vary \(x\) in any way: we could let it be \(1\), or \(-2\), or \(3\). But Lagrange is trying to minimize some function \(S\) which depends on functions like \(\vec x_1(t)\). That means we are free to choose each of the values \[ \vec x_1(0.01),\ \ \vec x_1(0.02),\ \ \vec x_1(0.03)\] freely! You could let \[ \vec x_1(0.01)=2.5,\ \ \vec x_1(0.02)=-\pi,\ \ \vec x_1(0.03)=20\] or \[ \vec x_1(0.01)=-0.7,\ \ \vec x_1(0.02)=-5,\ \ \vec x_1(0.03)=-2,\] or, or, or, ... It seems almost impossible to minimize a function over such a big space of possibilities!

Before trying to minimize the action, let's review how we minimize usual functions.

How to minimize a function

How do you minimize a function? Let's say we're trying to minimize the function \(f(x)=x^3-x^3-x+2\). Suppose \(f(x)\) achieved a minimum at some number \(x_0\), and let's try nudging it a little. Then \[ f(x_0+\epsilon)\approx f(x_0)+f'(x_0)\epsilon. \] If the derivative \(f'(x_0)\) were negative, then \[ f(x_0+\epsilon)\approx f(x_0)+f'(x_0)\epsilon \] would be a little smaller than \(f(x_0)\), which contradicts \(f(x_0)\) being the minimum. Similarly, if \(f'(x_0)\) were \[ f(x_0-\epsilon)\approx f(x_0)-f'(x_0)\epsilon \] would be a little smaller than \(f(x_0)\), which again contradicts \(f(x_0)\) being the minimum. That means for \(f\) to achieve a minimum at \(x_0\) we need \(f'(x_0)=0\).
