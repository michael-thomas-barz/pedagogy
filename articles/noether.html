Mechanics II: symmetry is conservation

August 22, 2026

Conservation laws are very important in physics. Let's review three key examples.

Conservation of energy

Let's imagine I let go of a ball of mass \(m\) at height \(h\). Initially, since the ball is not moving, there is no kinetic energy. However, there is a potential energy of \(mgh\) coming from gravity.

After some time \(t\), the ball has fallen to some height \(h'\). Since the gravitational acceleration is \(g\), the ball is now falling at speed \(gt\). So now the ball has kinetic energy of \[ \frac12 mv^2=\frac12m(gt)^2, \] and has potential energy \(mgh'\). By conservation of energy, we know \[ mgh=\frac12m(gt)^2+mgh', \] and we can solve for \(h'\): \[ h'=h-\frac12gt^2. \] Of course, if you wanted to solve this particular problem, you could have also done a little calculus. But conservation of energy alone can get you pretty far!

Conservation of momentum

Now, imagine I am playing billiard. A billiard ball of mass \(m_1\) is sitting on the table, and I hit it with another billiard ball of \(m_2\), moving at velocity \(v\). What happens to the balls?

Before the balls collide, my ball has mass \(m_2\) and velocity \(v\) so the momentum is \(m_2v\). Since the other billiard ball is just sitting, it has no momentum.

After the collision, the billiard ball I hit is moving at another velocity \(v_2\), and the billiard ball which was sitting on the table is now moving at some velocity \(v_1\). The total momentum here is \(m_1v_1+m_2v_2\).

By conservation of momentum, we know the total momentum is conserved, so \[ m_2v=m_1v_1+m_2v_2. \] If you combine this with the conservation of energy, \[ \frac12m_2v^2=\frac12m_1v_1^2+\frac12m_2v_2^2, \] you can solve for \(v_1\) and \(v_2\)!

Conservation of angular momentum

Angular momentum is defined with respect to some origin. For a particle with momentum \(\vec p\) and position vector \(\vec r\), the angular momentum is the cross product \(\vec r\times\vec p\).

For example, say there is a particle of mass \(m\) rotating in a circle of radius \(r\) around a rod with frequency \(f\). Then the particle is moving with velocity \(2\pi fr\), so the momentum is \(2\pi mfr\). Therefore, the angular momentum has size \[2\pi mfr^2.\] Let's say the particle moves into a smaller orbit, of radius \(r'<{r}\). That particle will have a new frequency \(f'\). Then conservation of angular momentum tells us \[2\pi mfr^2=2\pi mf'(r')^2.\] We can solve for \(f'\) as \[f'=\frac{r^2}{(r')^2}f>f.\] In other words, as the radius decreases, the frequency increases! This is the reason why when figure skaters pull in their arms, they rotate more frequently.

We see that each of our conserved quantities (energy, momentum, and angular momentum) tells us something useful about our physical system. However, finding these quantities requires some cleverness on our part. Wouldn't it be wonderful if there is a more systematic method for finding conserved quantities? Emmy Noether discovered that conserved quantities exactly correspond to continuous symmetries of the physical system, thereby answering this question!

What is continuous symmetry?

A windmill as below has symmetries. For example, rotating the picture by \(90^\circ\) doesn't change the picture. Neither does rotation by \(180^\circ\) or \(270^\circ\). Of course rotating by \(0^\circ\), which does nothing, also doesn't change the shape! However, if you rotate by, say, \(30^\circ\), the blades will be at a different position. So we would say the windmill has 4 symmetries: rotation by \(0^\circ\), \(90^\circ\), \(180^\circ\), and \(270^\circ\).

On the other hand, consider a circle.

Then, rotating by any angle \(\theta\) doesn't change the shape. That means the circle has infinitely many symmetries! That's why we would say a circle has a continuous set of symmetries, while the windmill only has a discrete set of symmetries.

Our world has three types of continuous symmertries:

  • Time symmetry: our universe will have the same physical laws 10 seconds from now.
  • Translation symmetry: the laws of physics on my block is the same as the laws of physics 3 blocks down.
  • Rotational symmetry: if I turn left, the laws of physics don't change.
Noether tells us that each of these symmetries corresponds to a conserved quantity.

Let's explore how continuous symmetries really correspond to conserved quantities. We will start with conservation of energy.

Why is energy conserved?

Last week we discussed Lagrange's approach to mechanics. Let's understand why energy is conserved from this perspective.

As a quick review, in Lagrange's approach, we take a function called the Lagrangian \(L(\vec x,\vec v,t)=T-V\), and try to minimize the action integral \[ S=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}L\big(\vec x(t),\vec v(t),t\big)dt. \] Minimizing this integral amounts to the Euler-Lagrange equation, \[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \vec x}=\frac d{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \vec v}. \] We want to find a conserved quantity. This is some function \(C\) which remains constant as our system evolves. Mathematically, this just means we want to find a function \(C\) such that \[ \frac{dC}{dt}=0. \]

Noether tells us that for convervation of energy, we need the Lagrangian to be independent of time; in other words, that our physical system obeys the "same laws" at every instance. There are two main reasons why this assupmtion may fail in general:

  1. the force on your system may depend on time; and
  2. the presence of non-conservative forces, such as friction, would make your Lagrangian look like \(e^{ct}L'\) where \(L'\) is some time-independent Lagrangian.

Now, since we have a function \(L\) lying around, let's just try to take its derivative! This can be computed by a multi-variable version of the chain rule, \[ \frac{dL}{dt}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\vec x}\cdot\frac{d\vec x}{dt}+\frac{\partial L}{\partial\vec v}\cdot\frac{d\vec v}{dt}+\frac{\partial L}{\partial t}. \] However under our assumption that \(L\) is time-independent the last term vanishes, and simply \[ \frac{dL}{dt}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\vec x}\cdot\frac{d\vec x}{dt}+\frac{\partial L}{\partial\vec v}\cdot\frac{d\vec v}{dt}. \] Now the Euler-Lagrange equation (and since \(\vec v=\frac{d\vec x}{dt}\) by definition) lets us re-write the first part: \[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial\vec x}\cdot\frac{d\vec x}{dt}+\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}\cdot\frac{d\vec v}{dt} =\frac d{dt}\bigg(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \vec v}\bigg)\cdot\vec v+\frac{\partial L}{\partial\vec v}\cdot\frac{d\vec v}{dt}. \] By the product rule, we can re-write this expression as \[ \frac d{dt}\bigg(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \vec v}\bigg)\cdot\vec v+\frac{\partial L}{\partial\vec v}\cdot\frac{d}{dt}\vec v=\frac d{dt}\bigg(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\vec v}\cdot\vec v\bigg). \] From our computations we conclude \[ \frac{dL}{dt}=\frac d{dt}\bigg(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\vec v}\cdot\vec v\bigg). \] This gives us a function \[ E=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\vec v}\cdot\vec v-L \] whose time-derivative is zero. This is a conserved quantity, which we will call energy.

Example: in Newtonian mechanics

Let's see what the conservation of energy means when our Lagrangian is of the form \[ L(\vec x,\vec v)=\frac12m\|\vec v\|^2-V(\vec x), \] where \(V(\vec x)\) is the potential energy of the particle \(\vec x\). As we computed last week, by the product rule \[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \vec v}=\frac\partial{\partial\vec v}\bigg(\frac12m\|\vec v\|^2\bigg)=m\vec v. \] Thus the energy becomes \[ E=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\vec v}\cdot\vec v-L=m\vec v\cdot\vec v-L=\frac12m\|\vec v\|^2+V(\vec x). \] That is, energy is the sum of kinetic energy and potential energy!

Side-quest: what about time-dependent Lagrangians?

By the way, what would happen if the Lagrangian \(L\) really depended on the time \(t\)? In this scenario, the time-derivative of \(L\) obtains a new term: \[ \frac{dL}{dt}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\vec x}\cdot\frac{d\vec x}{dt}+\frac{\partial L}{\partial\vec v}\cdot\frac{d\vec v}{dt}+\frac{\partial L}{\partial t}. \] The extra term \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial t}\), which measures "how much the physical laws of our system depends on time", will tag along through our entire derivation. So we would only be able to conclude that \[ \frac{dE}{dt}=-\frac{\partial L}{\partial t}. \] In other words, the conservation of energy (\(\frac{dE}{dt}=0\)) is equivalent to the time-independence of the Lagrangian (\(\frac{\partial L}{\partial t}=0\))! For example, when there is friction and our Lagrangian is of the form \(e^{ct}L'\) where \(L'\) is a time-independent Lagrangian. In this case, we see \[ \frac{dE}{dt}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\big(e^{ct}L'\big)=-ce^{ct}L'=-cL. \] This means energy is lost through friction, which matches our physical understanding.

Why is momentum conserved?

What exactly happened in the last section? We assumed that the Lagrangian is time-independent, and we analyzed what happens when we change time infinitesimally. Now, let's assume the Lagrangian \(L\) is translation-invariant, and analyze what happens under an infinitesimal change in position.

Before that, note that although above we only treated the Lagrangian for one particle, we could have just as easily treated the Lagrangian with \(n\) particles. In that case, we have a function \(L(\vec x_1,\dots,\vec x_n,\vec v_1,\dots,\vec v_n,t)\) depending on the position and velocities of \(n\) particles and time. The Euler-Lagrange equations becomes \(n\) equations \[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \vec x_1}=\frac d{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \vec v_1} \] \[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \vec x_2}=\frac d{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \vec v_2} \] \[ \cdots \] \[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \vec x_n}=\frac d{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \vec v_n}. \]

Now, let's shift our entire physical system by some small vector \(\vec\epsilon\). Then the Lagrangian changes as \[ L(\vec x_1+\vec\epsilon,\dots,\vec x_n+\vec\epsilon,\vec v_1,\dots,\vec v_n,t)=L+\frac{\partial L}{\partial \vec x_1}\cdot\vec\epsilon+\cdots+\frac{\partial L}{\partial \vec x_n}\cdot\vec\epsilon. \] In other words, the total change is \[ \bigg(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \vec x_1}+\cdots+\frac{\partial L}{\partial\vec x_n}\bigg)\cdot\vec\epsilon. \] If the Lagrangian is independent of such shifts, we must necessarily have \[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \vec x_1}+\cdots+\frac{\partial L}{\partial\vec x_n}=0. \] By the Euer-Lagrange equation, this means \[ \frac d{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\vec v_1}+\cdots+\frac d{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\vec v_n}=0. \] In other words, the function \[ C=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\vec v_1}+\cdots+\frac{\partial L}{\partial\vec v_n} \] satisfies \[ \frac{dC}{dt}=0,\] i.e., is a conserved quantity. Each derivative \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \vec v_i}\) is called the momentum of the \(i\)-th particle, and this exactly says the total momentum is conserved.

What would Newton say?

Let's again go back to the situation where the \(i\)-th particle has mass \(m_i\), and the Lagrangian has the form \[ L=\frac12m_1\|\vec v_1\|^2+\cdots+\frac12m_n\|\vec v_n\|^2-V(\vec x_1,\dots,\vec x_n) \] where \(V(\vec x_1,\dots,\vec x_n)\) is the sum of the potential energy of the particles. Then the momentum of the \(i\)-th particle is \[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial\vec v_i}=m_i\vec v_i, \] so the conservation of momentum says \[ m_1\vec v_1+\cdots+m_n\vec v_n \] is conserved. In other words, its time-derivative is zero: \[ m_1\vec a_1+\cdots+m_n\vec a_n=0.\] Newton tells us \(\vec F_i=m_i\vec a_i\) is the force the \(i\)-th particle exerts. So the conservation of momentum just says \[ \vec F_1+\cdots+\vec F_n=0. \] This is exactly Newton's third law, that every action has an equal and opposite reaction!

Why is angular momentum conserved?

The last conservation law--the conservation of angular momentum--is more involved, so we won't go through the full derivation. But the basic idea remains the same. Let's suppose our Lagrangian \[ L(\vec x_1,\dots,\vec x_n,\vec v_1,\dots,\vec v_n,t) \] is invariant under rotation. In the language of linear algebra, we can represent rotation by a 3x3-matrix \(A\). The invariance of the Lagrangian means \[ L(A\cdot \vec x_1,\dots,A\cdot \vec x_n,A\cdot \vec v_1,\dots,A\cdot\vec v_n,t)=L(\vec x_1,\dots,\vec x_n,\vec v_1,\dots,\vec v_n,t). \] In other words, the derivative by \(A\) (whatever that means...) is zero! \[ \frac d{dA}L(A\cdot \vec x_1,\dots,A\cdot \vec x_n,A\cdot \vec v_1,\dots,A\cdot\vec v_n,t)=0. \] Using the Euler-Lagrange equations, this expression can be re-written as \(\frac{dC}{dt}\) for some function \(C\). So we conclude \[ \frac{dC}{dt}=0, \] i.e., \(C\) is a conserved quantity. We call this quantity (really, a vector) \(C\) the angular momentum.

Conclusion: Lie groups

Noether's big insight was that continuous symmetries correspond to conservation laws. That means, whereas Newton needed to rely on some cleverness to discover conserved quantities, we can instead just hunt for symmetries of our physical system.

Sophus Lie took this idea of continuous symmetries and developed the theory of Lie groups. Lie groups are analogous to Galois's theory of symmetries that he developed in the course of studying solutions to equations, but encorporates what it means for two symmetries to be "close to each other." He then formalized how to perform calculus on Lie groups--how do you take derivatives of a function on a Lie group? This is precisely what we were doing when we took a derivative with respect to a matrix \(A\) in the derivation of angular momentum.

Sophus Lie's theory of continuous symmetries has since then developed into a rich subject now known as representation theory. Some questions are: What are all possible Lie groups (=sets of continuous symmetries)? In what dimensions can they be realized? What do they look like geometrically--we saw the example of a circle, but what about in higher dimensions?
