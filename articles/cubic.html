How to solve cubic and quartic equations II: cubics

July 24, 2026

Last week in How to solve cubic and quartic equations I: quartics we saw how to reduce the quartic equation to the cubic equation. This was done by carefully analyzing the symmetries of the solutions to the equation. Today, our question is: how do we solve cubic equations--equations that look like \[ x^3-7x^2+5x-20=0? \] Our approach will be to build an analogy with the solution to the quadratic equation.

Quadratic equations: yet again

We will highlight two techniques we can use to solve the quadratic equation \[ x^2+bx+c=0. \] Since the cubic equation is much more complicated, we will need to exploit both techniques.

Completing the square

One approach to the quadratic equation, completing the square, observes \[ \big(x+\frac b2\big)^2=x^2+bx+\frac{b^2}4 \] looks suspiciously similar to the equation we want to solve. We could re-write our original equation as \[ x^2+bx+c=\big(x^2+bx+\frac{b^2}4\big)+\big(c-\frac{b^2}4\big)=\big(x+\frac b2\big)^2+\big(c-\frac{b^2}4\big)=0. \] This tells us \[ \big(x+\frac b2\big)^2=\frac{b^2}4-c, \] and by taking square roots you can solve for \(x\).

Another way to think about completing the square is that the substitution \(x=y-\frac b2\) simplifies the equation. The equation \(x^2+bx+c=0\) then becomes \[ \big(y-\frac b2\big)^2+b\big(y-\frac b2\big)+c=0. \] If we expand each term: \[ \big(y-\frac b2\big)^2=y^2-by+\frac{b^2}4 \] \[ b\big(y-\frac b2\big)=by-\frac{b^2}2 \] \[ c=c\] and add everything up, we arrive at the quadratic equation \[ y^2+\big(c-\frac{b^2}4\big)=0. \] So the main point is that by our change of variables we were able to get rid of the linear term in our equation!

Exploiting symmetry

Let's now review the approach we used in How to solve cubic and quartic equations I: quartics.

Let's call the two solutions of the quadratic equation as \(\alpha\) and \(\beta.\) Then Vieta's formulas tell us \[\alpha + \beta = -b,\] \[\alpha\beta = c,\] and we could use these to find evaluate any symmetric polynomial of \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) (so computing \(\alpha^2 + \beta^2\) in terms of \(b\) and \(c\) is easy, for example).

To solve for \(\alpha, \beta,\) we needed to evaluate some quantity which broke the symmetry. The quantity we chose was \(\alpha - \beta.\) This was not symmetric in \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) -- because swapping the two roots negates this expression -- but \((\alpha-\beta)^2\) was symmetric. This meant that we could write \((\alpha-\beta)^2\) in terms of \(b\) and \(c\), as \[(\alpha-\beta)^2 = b^2 - 4c,\] and then taking a square root gives us the asymmetric quantity \(\alpha - \beta.\)

But how did we know to use \(\alpha - \beta\)? One way of thinking about it is that this quantity has the same symmetry as the square root function. What do we mean by this?

Well, the quadratic formula is going to have to involve the square root function. And square roots always come in positive/negative pairs: the equation \(y^2 = 4\) has solutions \(y = 2\) and \(y = -2.\)

In our final square root formula, we write \[\alpha - \beta = \sqrt{b^2 - 4c}.\] The \(\pm\) ambiguity in the square root on the right hand side is reflected in the \(\pm\) ambiguity in the expression \(\alpha-\beta\) on the left hand side: depending on which root we call \(\alpha\) and which we call \(\beta,\) the expression \(\alpha - \beta\) could take on two possible values, which are negatives of each other, just like how the square root can be either positive or negative.

To the cubic

We now want to derive a cubic formula, to solve equations \[x^3 + ax^2 + bx + c = 0.\]

The cubic formula will almost certainly involve the cube root function. However, it will also involve square roots. To see why, notice that we can turn any quadratic equation into a cubic equation: the equation \(x^2 - 2 = 0\) can be multiplied by \(x,\) transforming it into \[x^3 - 2x = 0.\]

The solutions to this cubic are \(x=0\), \(x=\sqrt{2},\) and \(x=-\sqrt{2}.\) So, if we are going to have a general cubic formula, it will need to involve square roots.

In the quadratic case, we figured out what quantity to take the square root of by trying to find some quantity which was multiplied by \(-1\) when we permuted the roots. Our game plan for the cubic will be to first figure out what quantity we should take the square root of, then figure out what we will take the cube root of.

But before doing that, we do one small step to make the algebra easier in our work below; this step isn't essential, but it makes everything quicker. Similar to the completing-the-square trick for quadratic equations, if we substitute \(x=y-a/3\) in the cubic \[x^3 +ax^2 + bx + c = 0,\] we can get rid of the quadratic term to an equation of the form \[ y^3+py+q=0. \]

You can check out the full computation here.

After substituting \(x=y-a/3\) our equation becomes \[(y -\frac a3)^3 + a(y -\frac a3)^2 + b(y -\frac a3) + c = 0.\] At first, this doesn't seem any simpler, but observe that if we expand this out, we get \[(y-\frac a3)^3 = y^3 - ay^2 + \frac{a^2}{3}y - \frac{a^3}{27},\] \[a(y-\frac a3)^2 = ay^2 - \frac{2a^2}{3}y + \frac{a^3}{9},\] \[b(y -\frac a3) = by - \frac{ab}{3},\] \[c = c,\] so adding these together, we can rewrite \[(y-\frac a3)^3 + a(y -\frac a3)^2 + b(y-\frac a3) + c = 0\] as \[y^3 + \left(b- \frac{a^2}{3}\right)y + \left(\frac{2a^3}{27} - \frac{ab}{3} + c\right) = 0.\]

If we set \(p = b - a^2/3\) and \(q = 2a^3/27 - ab/3 + c,\) then this becomes \[y^3 + py + q = 0.\] This cubic equation has no \(y^2\) term, and hence is a bit easier to manipulate. We will use it below.

So, take the cubic equation \[y^3 + py + q = 0\] and label its roots \(y = \alpha\), \(y = \beta,\) and \(y = \gamma.\) We will now find an equation for these roots.

The quadratic part of the cubic formula

Recalling our plan from earlier, we start by figuring out what we should take the square root of. This means finding some quantity in terms of \(\alpha, \beta,\) and \(\gamma\) which isn't symmetric, but only in a mild way: relabelling the roots should at worst replace this quantity with its negative.

Let's copy what we did for the quadratic and consider the quantity \(\alpha - \beta.\) When we swap the values of \(\alpha\) and \(\beta,\) this becomes \(\beta - \alpha,\) the negative of itself. Off to a good start!

But now that we have three roots, we have six different ways to permute them.

For example, the cubic \(x^3 - 3x + 1 = 0\) has solutions \(x \approx -1.88, x \approx 0.35, x \approx 1.53.\) Then, there are six ways of deciding how to call these roots \(\alpha,\beta,\) and \(\gamma.\)

\( \alpha = -1.88,\\ \beta = 0.35,\\ \gamma = 1.53 \) \( \alpha = -1.88,\\ \beta = 1.53,\\ \gamma = 0.35 \) \( \alpha = 0.35,\\ \beta = -1.88,\\ \gamma = 1.53 \)
\( \alpha = 0.35,\\ \beta = 1.53,\\ \gamma = -1.88 \) \( \alpha = 1.53,\\ \beta = -1.88,\\ \gamma = 0.35 \) \( \alpha = 1.53,\\ \beta = 0.35,\\ \gamma = -1.88 \)

And while \(\alpha - \beta\) transforms well when we swap \(\alpha\) and \(\beta,\) if we had instead swapped \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\) then something bad would happen: \(\alpha - \beta\) would become \(\alpha - \gamma,\) which is completely unrelated to \(\alpha - \beta.\)

Fortunately, there's a fix. Consider the product \[D = (\alpha - \beta)(\beta - \gamma)(\gamma - \alpha).\]

If we swap \(\alpha\) and \(\beta,\) then this turns into \[(\beta - \alpha)(\alpha - \gamma)(\gamma - \beta).\] Notice that this new product is equal to \((-1)^3 \cdot D = -D,\) because if we negate each factor of this product, we get \(D.\)

Similarly, if we swap \(\alpha\) and \(\gamma\) or if we swap \(\beta\) and \(\gamma,\) then \(D\) becomes \(-D.\) We're off to a good start!

Those `root swaps' correspond to three different labellings of our roots; if we count the original \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\) way, then that's four permutations accounted for. How about the last two?

We could also cycle the values; for example, going back to that cubic \(x^3 - 3x + 1 = 0\) from above, if we started with \[\alpha = -1.88, \beta = 0.35, \gamma = 1.53,\] then we could move each value forward one to relabel them as \[\alpha = 1.53, \beta = -1.88, \gamma = 0.35.\] Notice how the new value of \(\alpha\) is the old \(\gamma\); the new value of \(\beta\) is the old \(\alpha\); and the new \(\gamma\) is the old \(\beta.\) Thus, under this type of permutation, \(D = (\alpha-\beta)(\beta-\gamma)(\gamma-\alpha)\) becomes \[(\gamma - \alpha)(\alpha - \beta)(\beta - \gamma),\]4 which is actually equal to \(D\) itself!

Similarly, we could cycle the values the other way, relabelling \[\alpha = -1.88, \beta = 0.35, \gamma = 1.53\] as \[\alpha = 0.35, \beta = 1.53, \gamma = -1.88.\] Under this relabelling, \(D\) also is unchanged!

We now have this quantity \(D\) which has the property that, when we permute the values \(\alpha, \beta,\) and \(\gamma,\) the quantity \(D\) is either unchanged or multiplied by \(-1.\)

In particular, \(D^2\) is a symmetric function of \(\alpha,\beta,\) and \(\gamma\) -- if we relabel the values of \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\) then \(D^2\) always stays the same, because the relabelling can at worst change us from \(D^2\) to \((-D)^2,\) but \(D^2 = (-D)^2.\)

Thus, as we discussed last week, the symmetric function \(D^2\) can be written in terms of the coefficients \(p,q\) of our cubic equation \[y^3 + py + q = 0.\] As we show below, it turns out that \[D^2 = -4p^3 - 27q^2.\]

If you click here, you can see the full derivation of the formula \(D^2 = -4p^3 - 27q^2,\) but its somewhat tedious algebra, so feel free to skip it if you desire.

Recall from Vieta's formulas (discussed last week) that \[\alpha + \beta + \gamma = 0,\] \[\alpha\beta + \beta\gamma + \gamma\alpha = p,\] \[\alpha\beta\gamma = -q.\]

Recall that \[D^2 = (\alpha-\beta)^2(\beta-\gamma)^2(\gamma-\alpha)^2.\] The name of the game is subtracting away polynomials in \(p\) and \(q\) from \(D^2\) until we get 0.

When we expand this out, \(D^2\) will get a term of the form \(\alpha^2 \cdot \beta^2 \cdot (-\alpha)^2 = \alpha^4\beta^2.\) Let's try to kill that term.

This term \(\alpha^4\beta^2\) has symmetric partners: the expansion of \(D^2\) also contains terms \(\alpha^4\gamma^2, \beta^4\alpha^2, \beta^4\gamma^2, \gamma^4\alpha^2,\) and \(\gamma^4\beta^2.\) So, let's try to kill all of these terms at once; this amounts to rewriting \[\alpha^4(\beta^2 + \gamma^2) + \beta^4(\alpha^2 + \gamma^2) + \gamma^4(\alpha^2 + \beta^2)\] in terms of \(p\) and \(q.\)

Observe first that \[0^2 = (\alpha + \beta + \gamma)^2 = (\alpha^2 + \beta^2 + \gamma^2) + 2(\alpha\beta + \beta\gamma + \gamma\alpha),\] so that \[\alpha^2 + \beta^2 + \gamma^2 = -2p.\]

Thus \[\alpha^4(\beta^2 + \gamma^2) + \beta^4(\alpha^2 + \gamma^2) + \gamma^4(\alpha^2 + \beta^2)\] can be rewritten as \[\alpha^4(-2p - \alpha^2) + \beta^4(-2p - \beta^2) + \gamma^4(-2p - \gamma^2),\] which can further be rewritten as \[-2p(\alpha^4 + \beta^4 + \gamma^4) - (\alpha^6 + \beta^6 + \gamma^6).\]

As \[\alpha^2 + \beta^2 + \gamma^2 = -2p,\] we can square it to derive \[(\alpha^4 + \beta^4 + \gamma^4) + 2(\alpha^2\beta^2 + \beta^2\gamma^2 + \gamma^2\alpha^2) = 4p^2.\] Note \[(\alpha\beta + \beta\gamma + \gamma\alpha)^2 = (\alpha^2\beta^2 + \beta^2\gamma^2 + \gamma^2\alpha^2) + 2\alpha\beta\gamma(\alpha + \beta + \gamma),\] so that \[\alpha^4 + \beta^4 + \gamma^4 = 4p^2 - 2p^2 = 2p^2.\]

Now, we just need to figure out \(\alpha^6 + \beta^6 + \gamma^6.\) Observe first that in \[0^3 = (\alpha + \beta + \gamma)^3\] we can expand the right hand side to get \[0 = (\alpha^3 + \beta^3 + \gamma^3) + (\alpha + \beta + \gamma)(\alpha^2 + \beta^2 + \gamma^2 -\alpha\beta - \beta\gamma - \gamma\alpha) - 3\alpha\beta\gamma,\] so that \[0 = (\alpha^3 + \beta^3 + \gamma^3) + 0 + 3q,\] or \(\alpha^3 + \beta^3 + \gamma^3 = -3q.\)

Squaring this, we find \[(\alpha^6 + \beta^6 + \gamma^6) + 2(\alpha^3\beta^3 + \beta^3\gamma^3 + \gamma^3\alpha^3) = 9q^2.\]

The upshot to this is that we should expect the cubic formula will involve the quantity \[\sqrt{-4p^3 - 27q^2}.\]

There are two possible choices of that square root; they correspond to the two possible values of \(D\) (this \(D\) has two values because, depending on how you label the roots \(\alpha, \beta,\) and \(\gamma,\) this \(D\) could take on two opposite values).

In particular, if we choose one of the values of that square root, then we eliminate some symmetry. This is just like what happened when we chose a square root when solving the quadratic case: if we choose a square root, and set \(D\) equal to that square root, then we can no longer swap two roots, because swapping two roots would negate our value of \(D,\) but now we've fixed \(D.\) Observe how by choosing \(D,\) we've reduced the symmetry.

However, we haven't gotten rid of all the symmetries: while the root swapping symmetries go away, above we saw that those two `cycle the roots' symmetries don't change \(D\) at all. Thus, we now have three valid permutations of \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma\) remaining.

The cube root in the cubic formula

The cubic formula also has to involve a cube root. So, let's ask ourselves: which quantity should we be taking the cube root of?

Well, let's take a step back. It is well known that square roots come in pairs. But how many solutions to \(z^3 = 1\) are there, say?

We can rewrite this equation as \[z^3 - 1 = 0,\] and then factor it as \[(z-1)(z^2 + z + 1) = 0.\]

The quadratic equation \(z^2 + z + 1 = 0\) has solutions \[z = \frac{-1 \pm \sqrt{-3}}{2}.\]

People often use the symbol \(\omega\) (omega) to denote the number \[\omega = \frac{-1 + \sqrt{-3}}{2}.\] Notice that \(\omega\) is a complex number, because \(\sqrt{-3}\) is imaginary. For those who know Euler's formula, we remark that \(\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}.\)

The other root of \(z^2 + z + 1 = 0\) is simply \(\omega^2\) (check for yourself!). Thus \(z^3 = 1\) has three solutions: \(1, \omega,\) and \(\omega^2.\) This means that every cube root problem has three solutions. For instance, \(3^3 = 27,\) but \(27\) also has two more cube roots: \[(3\omega)^3 = 3^3 \cdot \omega^3 = 27 \cdot 1 = 27\] and \[(3\omega^2)^3 = 3^3 \cdot (\omega^2)^3 = 27 \cdot 1 = 27.\]

So, while square roots come in pairs, cube roots come in triples \(r, r\omega, r\omega^2\).

When solving the quadratic equation, we tried to find some quantity which had the same symmetry as the square root function. Now, we want to find some expression in terms of \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma\) (the roots of our cubic) which has the symmetry, that when you permute the roots, the expression is multiplied by \(\omega\) or \(\omega^2.\)

When we wanted an expression which was multiplied by \(-1\) when we swapped the roots, we used \(\alpha + (-1)\cdot \beta.\) This inspires us to use the following trick: consider \[A = \alpha + \omega \cdot \beta + \omega^2 \cdot \gamma.\]

Recall the two cycling symmetries from above, where we cycle all the values around either left or right; under these cycling symmetries, the quantity \(A\) above transforms to either \[\gamma + \omega \cdot \alpha + \omega^2 \cdot \beta\] or \[\beta + \omega \cdot \gamma + \gamma^2 \cdot \alpha,\] depending on which way you cycle the values.

But recall that \(\omega^3 = 1,\) so that \[\omega \cdot A = \gamma + \omega \cdot \alpha + \omega^2 \cdot \beta\] and \[\omega^2 \cdot A = \beta + \omega \cdot \gamma + \gamma^2 \cdot \alpha.\]

This is great: it means that, under these cyclicing permutations, the quantity \(A\) gets multiplied by \(\omega\) or \(\omega^2\) -- this is exactly the way we'd hope a cube root gets changed!

The swapping permutations (where we swap the value of \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) and leave \(\gamma\) alone, for example) don't respect \(A\) at all. But remember: at the end of the last section, we chose a value of \(D,\) and so we no longer have to worry about these swapping permutations at all!

We have this quantity \(A,\) and we know that when we cycle the roots around, while the value of \(A\) may change, the quantity \(A^3\) never changes. Let's now try rewriting \(A^3\) in terms of \(p, q,\) and \(D.\) Using \(p\) and \(q\) isn't new to us at all: last week we saw that any symmetric polynomial of \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\) can be rewritten in terms of \(p, q.\) But \(A^3\) is not fully symmetric, since swapping two roots destroys its values. This is why we need the additional quantity \(D,\) which breaks the swapping root symmetries, to determine \(A^3.\)

Anyways, let's try computing. First, we can expand out \((\alpha + \omega\beta + \omega^2\gamma)^3\) to find the rather lengthy expression \[A^3 = (\alpha^3 + \beta^3 + \gamma^3) + 3\omega \cdot \alpha^2\beta + 3\omega^2 \cdot \alpha^2\gamma + 3\omega^2 \cdot \alpha\beta^2 + 3\omega \cdot \beta^2\gamma + 3\omega \cdot \alpha\gamma^2 + 3\omega^2 \cdot \beta\gamma^2 + 6\alpha\beta\gamma.\]

This is rather complicated, so let's start by getting rid of the sum \(\alpha^3 + \beta^3 + \gamma^3\) at the start of \(A^3.\) This is symmetric, so we can rewrite it in terms of \(p\) and \(q.\) In fact, in our derivation of \(D^2\) (which we collapsed to save space...) we already computed \[\alpha^3 + \beta^3 + \gamma^3 = -3q.\] The very last term of \(A^3\) was \(6\alpha\beta\gamma,\) which is just \(-6q,\) so we can rewrite \[A^3 = -9q + 3\omega(\alpha^2\beta + \beta^2\gamma + \gamma^2\alpha) + 3\omega^2(\alpha^2\gamma + \gamma^2\beta + \beta^2\alpha).\]

Set \[u = \alpha^2\beta + \beta^2\gamma + \gamma^2\alpha,\] \[v = \alpha^2\gamma + \gamma^2\beta + \beta^2\alpha.\] If we can write \(u\) and \(v\) in terms of \(p, q,\) and \(D,\) then we can do the same for \(A^3.\)

As \(u, v\) are no longer symmetric, we will really need to use \(D\) to express them. So, let's think about \[D = (\alpha - \beta)(\beta - \gamma)(\gamma - \alpha)\] for a moment. If we expand it out, we get \[D = \alpha^2(\gamma - \beta) + \beta^2(\alpha - \gamma) + \gamma^2(\beta - \alpha),\] so that actually \[D = v - u.\]

Observe also that \[u + v = \alpha^2(\beta + \gamma) + \beta^2(\alpha + \gamma) + \gamma^2(\alpha + \beta)\] is fully symmetric, so we can express it in terms of \(p\) and \(q.\) As \(\alpha + \beta + \gamma = 0,\) we can further rewrite \(u+v\) as \[u+v = -\alpha^3 - \beta^3 - \gamma^3.\] But a moment ago, we said \(\alpha^3 + \beta^3 + \gamma^3 = -3q,\) so that \[u+v = 3q.\] Thus we have two variables \(u\) and \(v,\) and two linear equations: \(v - u = D, v + u = 3q.\) We can solve this system to find \[v = \frac{D + 3q}{2},\] \[u = \frac{3q - D}{2}.\]

Therefore \[A^3 = -9q + \frac{3\omega}{2}(3q - D) + \frac{3\omega^2}{2}(3q + D),\] or even more simply \[A^3 = -\frac{27}{2}q - \frac{3i\sqrt{3}}{2}D.\]

In particular, if we choose a cube root root of \[-\frac{27}{2}q - \frac{3i\sqrt{3}}{2}D,\] then we'll have a value for \(A.\) Thus, we now know the value of \[\alpha + \omega\beta + \omega^2\gamma.\] We also know that \(\alpha + \beta + \gamma = 0.\) We have three variables, and two linear equations. We can almost solve for \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\) easily, but we need one more linear equation between them. Where can we get it?

The quantity \(A\) does not behave well when we swap the values of \(\alpha\) and \(\beta.\) Doing so leads us to the new value \[B = \beta + \omega\alpha + \omega^2\gamma.\] Note that, if we could determine the value of \(B\) (in terms of our known quantities \(p, q, D,\) and \(A\)), then we would get a third linear equation relating the varaibles \(\alpha,\beta,\) and \(\gamma.\) Three linear equations in three variables is easy to solve with linear algebra, so let's get going to determine \(B.\)

Fortunately, while \(A\) and \(B\) alone are not symmetric, the product \(AB\) is symmetric in \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma.\) Indeed, one can expand out \(AB\) to find \[AB = -\omega(\alpha\beta + \beta\gamma + \gamma\alpha) + \omega(\alpha^2 + \beta^2 + \gamma^2).\] As \[\alpha^2 + \beta^2 + \gamma^2 = (\alpha+\beta+\gamma)^2 - 2(\alpha\beta + \beta\gamma + \gamma\alpha),\] we find \[AB = -\omega p + \omega \cdot (0^2 - 2p) = -3\omega p.\]

So, we now know the value of \(B\): it is just \(-3\omega p/A.\)

Now, to find our roots! We have the three linear equations \[\alpha + \beta + \gamma = 0,\] \[\alpha + \omega\beta + \omega^2\gamma = A,\] and \[\omega\alpha + \beta + \omega^2\gamma = B.\]

If we multiply the second equation by \(\omega^2,\) we get \[\alpha + \omega^2\beta + \omega\gamma = \omega^2 B.\] Using that \(1 + \omega + \omega^2 = 0,\) adding up our equations gives \[3\alpha + 0\beta + 0\gamma = 0 + A + \omega^2B.\] Thus \[\alpha = \frac{A + \omega^2B}{3}.\]

As swapping \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) interchanges \(A\) and \(B,\) we deduce \[\beta = \frac{\omega^2A + B}{3},\] and therefore \[\gamma = \frac{\omega A + \omega B}{3}.\]

These are the three solutions to our cubic! Rewritten to eliminate \(B,\) we get \[\alpha = \frac{A}{3} - \frac{p}{A},\] \[\beta = \frac{\omega^2 A}{3} - \frac{\omega p}{A},\] \[\gamma = \frac{\omega A}{3} - \frac{\omega^2 p}{A}.\]

We remark that the system of equations we solved here may remind you of the discrete Fourier transform, which we discussed in Infinitely many primes end in a 7. And indeed, secretly the reason that this system of equations was so easy to solve was because of its relation to this discrete Fourier transform.

Using the cubic formula in practice

As our derivation of the cubic formula occupied many steps, let's use it on an explicit example now, just to make sure we understand what's going on. Consider the depressed cubic \[x^3 - 3x + 1 = 0.\] Earlier, we saw it had approximate solutions \(x \approx -1.88, x \approx 0.35, x \approx 1.53.\) Let's use our cubic formula to compute these roots exactly!

Here we have \(p = -3, q = 1.\) We start by needing to choose a square root of \[D^2 = -4p^3 - 27q^2 = -4 \cdot (-27) - 27 = 81.\] The number \(81\) has two square roots; let's go with the positive one, and choose \(D = 9\) for now.

We next need to choose a cube roots \[A^3 = -\frac{27}{2}q - \frac{3i\sqrt{3}}{2}D\]

Plugging in \(q = 1, D = 9,\) we find \[A^3 = \frac{-27 - 27i\sqrt{3}}{2} = 27\omega^2,\]

If we write \(\omega^2\) in polar form as \(\omega^2 = e^{4\pi i/3},\) we can deduce that one possible cube root of \(\omega\) is \[e^{4\pi i/9} = \cos\left(\frac{4\pi}{9}\right) + i\sin\left(\frac{4\pi}{9}\right).\]

So, we can choose \[A = 3e^{4\pi i/9} = 3\cos\left(\frac{4\pi}{9}\right) + 3i\sin\left(\frac{4\pi}{9}\right).\]

Plugging in these values into our cubic formula above, we get \[\alpha = e^{4\pi i/9} + e^{-4\pi i/9} = 2\cos\left(\frac{4\pi}{9}\right),\] \[\beta = e^{16\pi i/9} + e^{2\pi i/9} = 2\cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{9}\right),\] \[\gamma = e^{10\pi i/9} + e^{8\pi i/9} = 2\cos\left(\frac{8\pi}{9}\right).\]

With a calculator, we can check that \[2\cos(4\pi/9) \approx 0.35...,\] \[2\cos(2\pi/9) \approx 1.53...,\] \[2\cos(8\pi/9) \approx -1.88...,\] in agreement with the approximate solutions we found before!

We remark that there are, in general, two possible choices of \(D\) (the two different square roots) and three possible choices of \(A\) (the three different cube roots). This means there are \(2 \cdot 3 = 6\) possible choices we could have made; these choices correspond to the 6 different permutations of our roots. If we had chosen \(D = -9\) earlier, or if we had used a different cube root of \(27\omega^2,\) then we would have gotten the same roots, but we'd have assigned the labels \(\alpha,\beta,\) and \(\gamma\) differently.

Closing remarks

We have now derived the cubic formula, and checked that it worked on a concrete cubic polynomial.

In our derivation, we used the fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials: the fact that any symmetric polynomial in \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\) can be rewritten as a polynomial in terms of the elementary symmetric sums \(\alpha+\beta+\gamma, \alpha\beta+\beta\gamma+\gamma\alpha,\) and \(\alpha\beta\gamma.\)

At some point in our derivation, we also had to rewrite the non-symmetric polynomial \(A^3\) in terms of \(p, q,\) and \(D.\) This is not quite the fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials -- \(A^3\) is not symmetric, and \(D\) wasn't one of the elementary symmetric sums -- but it also isn't that far from it.

Galois theory is a vast generalization of the fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials; just as the fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials allows you to rewrite any symmetric polynomial in terms of simpler polynomials, Galois theory allows one to understand certain almost-symmetric polynomials in terms of more basic operations.

To develop Galois theory, one also needs to understand symmetries in a more tangible way. This leads to the concept of a group, a mathematical formalization of symmetry. A key insight of mathematics is that having the correct language to discuss an idea can often greatly simplify your work; for example, having the language of the polar form of a complex number made it much easier for us to compute the cube root of \(\omega^2\) earlier. The concept of a group has proven to be an incredibly useful language for describing symmetry, and having it is what gives Galois theory its power.
