How to solve cubic and quartic equations

July 10, 2026

We all learned the quadratic formula in school. It says that the solution to \(ax^2+bx+c=0\) is \[ x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}. \] How do you prove this? This can be done by several reduction steps. First of all, by dividing the equation out by \(a\) we arrive at \[ x^2+\frac bax+\frac ca=0, \] so we might as well have assumed \(a=1\) from the beginning. Next, in the equation \(x^2+bx+c=0\) we hope to get rid of the linear term. This is accomplished by the change of variables \(x=y-b/2\), which gives us \[ \bigg(y-\frac b2\bigg)^2+b\bigg(y-\frac b2\bigg)+c=0. \] When we expand out \[ \bigg(y-\frac b2\bigg)^2=y^2-by+\frac{b^2}4, \] we see the term \(-by\) exactly cancels out the linear term. Thus at the end our equation becomes \[ \bigg(y^2-by+\frac{b^2}4\bigg)+\bigg(by-\frac{b^2}2\bigg)+c=y^2+\bigg(c-\frac{b^2}4\bigg)=0. \] This tells us \[ y=\sqrt{\frac{b^2}4-c}=\frac{\sqrt{b^2-4c}}2.\] Remembering that \(x=y-b/2\) we recover the quadratic formula!

By the way, what does it mean to solve the quadratic equation \(x^2+bx+c=0\)? If we call the two solutions \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), we want to factor our polynomial as \[ x^2+bx+c=(x-\alpha)(x-\beta). \] By expanding and comparing coefficients, we see that we are looking for numbers \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) which satisfy \[ \alpha+\beta=-b,\ \alpha\beta=c. \] This perspective will help us later when we try to solve the cubic equation.

Cubic equations

Now, let's try to solve the cubic equation \(ax^3+bx^2+cx+d=0\). By the same trick as in the quadratic case, we may assume \(a=1\). Furthermore by substituting \(x=y-b/3\) we can get rid of the quadratic term, since \[ \bigg(y-\frac b3\bigg)^3=y^3-by^2+\frac{b^2}3y-\frac{b^3}{27}. \] So, let's just think about the cubic \[x^3+px+q=0.\] This is known as the depressed cubic, and people were stuck here for a while. But in the late 15th century, del Ferro and Tartaglia discovered a way to proceed. This is doubly impressive since they didn't even know about negative numbers at this time! Although they were the first to solve the cubic, since Cardano published it, the following is known as Cardano's formula.

They decided to substitute \(x=u+v\), which increases the number of variables. This is highly counter-intuitive: why would increasing the number of variables help you solve an equation?

But let's believe in del Ferro and Tartaglia and proceed. Expanding \[ (u+v)^3+p(u+v)+q=0 \] tells us \[ u^3+v^3+(3uv+p)(u+v)+q=0. \] The middle term looks the most difficult so let's get rid of it by assuming \(3uv=p\). This tells us \[ u^3+v^3+q=0.\] In other words, we want to find numbers \(u\) and \(v\) such that \(uv=p/3\) and \(u^3+v^3=-q\). Notice that this is very similar to the relationos that the solutions to the quadratic equation satisfied. If we cube the first equation we see \[ u^3v^3=\frac{p^3}{27},\ u^3+v^3=-q.\] Then \(u^3\) and \(v^3\) are the solutions to the quadratic equation \(w^3+qw+p^3/27=0\) which we know how to solve! We conclude \[ u^3=-\frac q2+\sqrt{\frac{q^2}4+\frac{p^3}{27}},\ v^3=-\frac q2-\sqrt{\frac{q^2}4+\frac{p^3}{27}} \] so we see that the solutions to the depressed cubic looks like \[ u+v=\sqrt[3]{-\frac q2+\sqrt{\frac{q^2}4+\frac{p^3}{27}}}+\sqrt[3]{-\frac q2-\sqrt{\frac{q^2}4+\frac{p^3}{27}}}. \] This gives three solutions total, since there are three cube roots of a number.

Quartic equations

Now, let's try to solve the quartic equation. Just as the cubic was solved by reducing to the quadratic, the quartic equation is solved by reducing to the cubic. This reduction was discovered by Ferrari in 1540. Let us solve the quartic \[ x^4+ax^3+bx^2+cx+d=0. \] Let's denote the solutions as \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\). Then each of the coefficients \(a,b,c,d\) are expressible as symmetric polynomials of \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\): \[ a=-(\alpha+\beta+\gamma+\delta),\ b=\alpha\beta+\alpha\gamma+\alpha\delta+\beta\gamma+\beta\delta+\gamma\delta,\cdots. \] Here, by symmetric we mean the polynomial doesn't change if we permute the letters \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\). For example the polynomial \(\alpha^2+\beta^2\) is not symmetric since if we exchange \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\) the polynomial changes to \(\alpha^2+\gamma^2\).

In fact, if a polynomial of \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\) is symmetric, it is also a polynomial of \(a,b,c,d\). For example \(\alpha^2+\beta^2+\gamma^2+\delta^2\) is symmetric, and indeed \[ \alpha^2+\beta^2+\gamma^2+\delta^2=a^2-2b. \] This means that we know the quantity \(\alpha^2+\beta^2+\gamma^2+\delta^2\), even though we don't yet know what each individul number \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\) is. This is true generally, that we know the value of any symmetric polynomial in \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\).

How do we leverage this to reduce our problem to a cubic equation? First let's look at the polynomials \[ \alpha\beta+\gamma\delta,\ \alpha\gamma+\beta\delta,\ \alpha\delta+\beta\gamma. \] Even though each individual polynomial is not symmetric, permuting the \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\) only permutes the three polynomials amongst itselves. That means the coefficients of the polynomial \[ \big(x-(\alpha\beta+\gamma\delta)\big)\big(x-(\alpha\gamma+\beta\delta)\big)\big(x-(\alpha\delta+\beta\gamma)\big) \] are symmetric. That means we know the coefficients of this polynomial in terms of the original coefficients \(a,b,c,d\), and we call it the resolvent cubic.

By solving the resolvent cubic using Cardano's method we know the numbers \(p=\alpha\beta+\gamma\delta\), \(q=\alpha\gamma+\beta\delta\), and \(r=\alpha\delta+\beta\gamma\). The explicit formula is terribly complicated so we won't write it down here, but nevertheless we can in principle!

Now, how do we write \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\) in terms of the numbers \(p,q,r\)? Well, we know \[ p=\alpha\beta+\gamma\delta,\ d=\alpha\beta\gamma\delta. \] By solving the relevant quadratic equation, we deduce \(\alpha\beta\) and \(\gamma\delta\). By repeating the same trick for \(q\) we deduce \(\alpha\gamma\) and \(\beta\delta\), and by repeating the trick for \(r\) we deduce \(\alpha\delta\) and \(\beta\gamma\).

Next, observe that \[ p+q=\alpha\beta+\gamma\delta+\alpha\gamma+\beta\delta=(\alpha+\delta)(\beta+\gamma). \] We also know \[ (\alpha+\delta)+(\beta+\gamma)=-a, \] so by solving the relevant quadratic equation again we deduce \(\alpha+\delta\) and \(\beta+\gamma\). Since we already knew \(\alpha\delta\) and \(\beta\gamma\), we again solve the relevant quadratic equations to deduce all four solutions \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\).

This lets us solve quartic equations to our heart's desire, but writing out the full formula in terms of the variables \(a,b,c,d\) will take ages!

Quintics and beyond

For a long time, people unsuccessfully tried to extend Cardano and Ferrari's method to solve quintic equations. In the 19th century Galois proved that there doesn't exist a general formula for the solutions to quintic equations and beyond!

Notice that the solution to the quartic equation involved analyzing the symmetries of the solutions. Galois systematically studied the symmetries to the solutions of an equation, and created the field we now know as Galois theory.

Galois's insight was that the symmetries of the solutions to the quintic equation was too complicated in a very precise sense. In fact so complicated that there couldn't exist a formula for the solutions!
